### C-1 (p19, Abstract) — Central canonical thesis (LOAD-BEARING; THE WAVE-2 SUMMIT CLAIM)
> "**The Architecture of Viability** offers a comprehensive framework for understanding and navigating complex systems, from biological organisms to entire civilizations. The book introduces a novel conceptual structure known as the **viability grammar**, which connects seven core primitives: **constraint, margin, state, disturbance, perception, regulation, and options**. These elements form the foundation for assessing the viability of systems across scales, whether in ecology, health, governance, or society."

### C-2 (p19, Abstract) — Multi-thinker integration (CANONICAL CITATION)
> "By integrating **Ostrom's principles of commons governance, Friston's active inference models, and the work of leading thinkers like McMurtry, Galtung, and Vervaeke**, this book provides both a theoretical foundation and practical strategies for **regenerative complexity, syndemic governance, and civilizational renewal**."

### C-3 (p20, Executive Summary) — Altimeter of Coherence (NOVEL DIAGNOSTIC INSTRUMENT)
> "By introducing the **Altimeter of Coherence**, the book offers a diagnostic tool for identifying when systems are losing coherence and offers actionable strategies for civilizational renewal."

### C-4 (p21, Ch1 opening) — Spencer-Brown primary distinction (LOAD-BEARING)
> "Every architecture of thought begins with a cut. Before there can be a system, an environment, a self, a world, an organism, a society, or a planet, there must be a distinction by which something is marked as this rather than that. The first act is not measurement, explanation, prediction, or control. It is distinction. A domain is brought forth when a boundary is drawn, when a difference is held, when an inside is separated from an outside, when a figure is distinguished from its ground."

### C-5 (p21, Ch1) — Spencer-Brown attribution
> "George Spencer-Brown's *Laws of Form* begins from this stark insight: 'Draw a distinction.' The mark is not merely a sign placed upon a pre-existing world. It is the operation by which a world of relevance becomes available."

### C-6 (p21, Ch1) — Civilizational diagnostic (LOAD-BEARING)
> "A civilization does not collapse only when its material systems fail. It begins to collapse when its distinctions no longer disclose the relations upon which its own viability depends."

### C-7 (p22, §1.1) — Boundary discipline definition
> "A viable boundary must distinguish without severing. It must preserve enough closure for identity and enough openness for exchange. If the boundary dissolves, the system loses coherence. If the boundary hardens absolutely, the system loses adaptability. Life exists between these extremes. It is neither fusion with the environment nor domination over it, but ongoing relational regulation across a boundary."

### C-8 (p22, §1.1, on bad distinctions) — Three bad distinction examples (LOAD-BEARING canonical examples)
> "When an economic system distinguishes 'resources' from 'life-ground,' it can count extraction as wealth while treating ecological degradation as an externality. When a health system distinguishes 'disease' from 'social condition,' it can treat pathology downstream while ignoring upstream causes. When a political system distinguishes 'security' from 'structural violence,' it can normalize coercion while failing to recognize the conditions that generate conflict. In each case, the distinction produces blindness. It marks what can be counted and leaves unmarked what must be preserved."

### C-9 (p23, §1.2) — System-environment relational definition
> "A system is not simply an object located inside a larger space. A system is a pattern of relations that maintains some degree of coherence across time. Its environment is not merely what surrounds it, but what perturbs, supports, constrains, nourishes, threatens, and enables it."

### C-10 (p23, §1.2) — Question-relative distinction discipline (LOAD-BEARING)
> "The same entity can be system in one domain and environment in another. A forest may be an ecosystem in ecological analysis, an economic resource in extractive accounting, a sacred place in an Indigenous cosmology, a carbon sink in climate policy, a watershed regulator in public health, and a habitat network in conservation biology. The forest is not reducible to any single distinction. Each distinction discloses some relations and conceals others."

### C-11 (p24, §1.3) — Externalization-as-deferred-collapse (LOAD-BEARING)
> "A viable inside cannot be maintained by degrading the outside on which it depends. This is one of the central principles of the architecture of viability. A system that preserves its internal order by displacing disorder into its environment may appear successful for a time. It may grow, accumulate, expand, and reproduce. Yet if its environment is the condition of its own continued existence, such success is pathological. It is not viability but **deferred collapse**."

### C-12 (p25, §1.4) — Maturana operational-closure canonical claim
> "Maturana's biology of cognition is decisive here. For Maturana, living systems are operationally closed but structurally coupled. They do not receive information as if the world were simply poured into them. Rather, perturbations from the environment trigger changes determined by the structure of the organism. The organism does not passively mirror the world. It enacts a domain of relevance through its own organization."

### C-13 (p31, Ch2 opening) — Seven primitives as minimal grammar (CANONICAL DEFINITION)
> "This chapter proposes seven primitives as the minimal grammar of viability: constraint, margin, state, disturbance, perception, regulation, and options. These are not arbitrary categories. They are the irreducible relational functions that appear whenever a system must maintain coherence under conditions of limitation, perturbation, uncertainty, and possibility."

### C-14 (p31, Ch2) — Per-primitive necessity (LOAD-BEARING canonical list)
> "A viable system must have constraints, because without structure there is no identity, pattern, or form. It must have margins, because without reserve there is no resilience. It must have state, because viability is always embodied in a present condition. It must encounter disturbance, because no living system exists in a world without perturbation. It must have perception, because it must detect what matters. It must have regulation, because it must respond. It must retain options, because it must adapt, learn, and transform."

### C-15 (p32, §2.1 Constraint) — Constraint as architecture of permitted possibility (LOAD-BEARING)
> "Constraint is therefore never neutral. It is the architecture of permitted possibility. Every viable system must be constrained, but pathological systems often confuse constraint with control. Control imposes order from above or outside. Constraint, in the deeper sense used here, structures the conditions under which coherent self-organization can occur. The difference is fundamental. A living system is not viable because every element is commanded. It is viable because its relations are constrained in ways that allow adaptive regulation."

### C-16 (p33, §2.2 Margin) — Margin definition + anti-optimization warning (LOAD-BEARING)
> "Margin is the space between present demand and maximum capacity. It is the buffer, reserve, slack, redundancy, or adaptive room that allows a system to absorb disturbance without losing coherence. [...] Margin is not inefficiency. This is one of the great errors of modern optimization. Systems designed only for maximum throughput, minimum cost, just-in-time delivery, lean staffing, and continuous growth may appear efficient under stable conditions. But they do so by consuming their own buffers. They remove the very slack that allows adaptation. They increase performance by reducing resilience. The result is a system that looks successful until it is disturbed."

### C-17 (p34, §2.3 State) — State as carrier of history
> "State is the reality of the system now. It carries history. It embodies prior regulation. It reveals accumulated stress. It discloses what has been preserved, what has been depleted, and what is approaching threshold. Yet state must not be confused with essence. A system's current state is not its destiny."

### C-18 (p35, §2.4 Disturbance) — Disturbance-as-teacher
> "Disturbance is therefore a teacher. It shows where the boundary is weak, where margins are thin, where perception is distorted, where regulation is delayed, and where options have narrowed."

### C-19 (p36, §2.5 Perception) — Perception-as-power (LOAD-BEARING; engages Galtung structural-violence)
> "Perception therefore depends not only on sensors, but on trust, categories, language, and power. Who is allowed to signal? Which signals count? Which forms of evidence are recognized? What is dismissed as anecdotal, emotional, political, unscientific, or inconvenient? What does the system refuse to know because knowing would require transformation? A civilization can be rich in data and poor in perception. It can measure endlessly while failing to notice what matters."

### C-20 (p37, §2.6 Regulation) — Regulation ≠ control (LOAD-BEARING)
> "Regulation is not control in the narrow sense. Control suggests command, domination, or forcing. Regulation in living systems is more subtle. It is the maintenance of coherence through feedback-sensitive adjustment. It may stabilize, adapt, reorganize, or transform depending on the state of the system and the nature of the disturbance."
