## Verbatim key claims (15 claims; selective-high-fidelity)

### C-1 (p5, Abstract — LOAD-BEARING) — Violence-as-downstream-misalignment reframe
> "Modern societies possess unprecedented technological power, yet remain vulnerable to systemic instability, conflict, and ecological degradation. Traditional analyses often treat violence and conflict as primary phenomena arising from political disagreement, ideological rivalry, or geopolitical competition. This paper advances an alternative systems interpretation: **violence is better understood as a downstream manifestation of deeper misalignments between civilizational institutions and the ecological life-support systems upon which societies depend.**"

### C-2 (p5, Abstract — LOAD-BEARING) — Violence-Viability Architecture definition
> "Building on Johan Galtung's violence triangle and John McMurtry's life-value onto-axiology, the paper introduces the concept of a **Violence–Viability Architecture**. This framework integrates ecological foundations, institutional governance, cultural narratives, and regulatory dynamics into a unified model explaining how civilizations maintain or lose stability."

### C-3 (p5, Abstract) — Three cultural attractors
> "Cultural attractors such as **Chosenness–Myth–Trauma, Dualism–Manichaeism–Armageddon, and Repression–Projection** are examined as narrative mechanisms that shape societal responses to systemic stress."

### C-4 (p5, Abstract — LOAD-BEARING) — Central thesis
> "The central thesis is that **the fundamental task of governance is not merely conflict management but the maintenance of alignment between institutions, culture, and the life-ground that sustains human life**."

### C-5 (p6, Exec Summary) — Two-tradition synthesis
> "Two intellectual traditions form its conceptual foundation. First, **Johan Galtung's theory of the violence triangle** demonstrates that visible acts of violence are typically the surface expression of deeper structural and cultural dynamics... Second, **John McMurtry's concept of life-value onto-axiology** identifies the objective foundation upon which all societies depend: the life-ground."

### C-6 (p6, Exec Summary — LOAD-BEARING) — Civilization-as-three-domain layered architecture (CLUSTER-CANONICAL primitive)
> "This paper synthesizes these traditions through a systems framework called the Violence–Viability Architecture. The framework conceptualizes civilization as a layered structure composed of **three interacting domains: the life-ground, institutional systems, and cultural narratives**. When these domains remain aligned, societies maintain resilience and stability. When misalignment emerges — through ecological degradation, institutional failure, or polarizing narratives — societies drift toward instability."

### C-7 (p6, Exec Summary) — Civilizational stability landscape concept
> "To visualize these dynamics, the paper introduces the concept of a **civilizational stability landscape**. In this landscape, civilizations occupy basins of stability when ecological systems function reliably, institutions maintain legitimacy, and cultural narratives support cooperation. However, pressures such as environmental degradation, inequality, and institutional rigidity can gradually push societies toward thresholds beyond which rapid systemic change becomes possible."

### C-8 (p7, Exec Summary — LOAD-BEARING) — Central conclusion: Governance-as-life-ground-maintenance
> "The central conclusion of the paper is that **the primary responsibility of governance is the maintenance of the life-ground**. Economic systems, political institutions, and cultural narratives ultimately function as regulatory mechanisms that organize human interaction with ecological systems."

### C-9 (p7, Exec Summary) — Governance-failure mode
> "When governance systems prioritize short-term economic gains, geopolitical competition, or ideological conflict at the expense of life-support systems, societies increase the risk of systemic instability."

### C-10 (p49, §7.1 — LOAD-BEARING) — Three-structural-layers definition (DISTINCTIVE substrate)
> "The Violence–Viability Architecture begins by recognizing that civilizations operate across **three primary structural layers: the ecological foundation, the institutional superstructure, and the cultural interpretive layer**. At the base lies the life-ground, consisting of ecological systems and physical infrastructures that provide the material conditions necessary for human life... Above this ecological base lies the institutional layer, composed of governance structures, economic systems, regulatory frameworks, and technological infrastructures... The third layer consists of cultural systems, including narratives, values, belief structures, and symbolic frameworks through which societies interpret their experiences."

### C-11 (p50, §7.2 — LOAD-BEARING) — Violence triangle re-located in architecture
> "Seen within the Violence–Viability Architecture, these forms of violence correspond to **failures within the regulatory alignment of the three civilizational layers**. Structural violence reflects institutional mismanagement of life-support systems. Cultural violence reflects narrative frameworks that legitimize or obscure these misalignments. Direct violence represents the eventual breakdown of social order when systemic pressures exceed the capacity of institutions to manage them."

### C-12 (p50, §7.3 — LOAD-BEARING) — Viability dynamics = feedback loops
> "**Viability dynamics refer to the feedback loops through which ecological conditions, institutional systems, and cultural narratives influence one another over time.** When these feedback loops reinforce alignment with the life-ground, societies tend to experience stability and resilience. When they amplify misalignment, systemic stress accumulates."

### C-13 (p51, §7.4) — Misalignment-deepening sequence (LOAD-BEARING — sequencing model)
> "When these elements diverge, however, instability begins to accumulate. Ecological degradation may undermine resource availability. Institutional systems may distribute resources unevenly or fail to adapt to changing conditions. Cultural narratives may intensify divisions or legitimize unsustainable practices. As these misalignments deepen, structural violence increases. Access to life necessities becomes more uneven, social trust declines, and institutional legitimacy erodes. Cultural narratives may become increasingly polarized, framing systemic challenges as moral or identity-based conflicts. **Direct violence often emerges only after these deeper processes have developed over extended periods.**"

### C-14 (p51, §7.5 — LOAD-BEARING) — Reframe of governance objective
> "The primary task of governance is not simply maintaining order or maximizing economic output. Instead, it is to **maintain alignment between institutional systems and the life-ground while cultivating cultural narratives that support this alignment**."

### C-15 (TOC + Key Concepts, p93) — 4-regulatory-layer scheme (LOAD-BEARING; recurrent across trilogy)
> "Regulatory Layers of Civilizational Viability" — **Homeostasis / Allostasis / Adaptation / Reflexivity** [enumerated in TOC §4.4 + Key Concepts pp 92-93; biological-systems vocabulary extended to civilizational scale; parallels Wave-5 #1 5-stage Planetary Solvency transition but at finer-grained regulatory scope].
