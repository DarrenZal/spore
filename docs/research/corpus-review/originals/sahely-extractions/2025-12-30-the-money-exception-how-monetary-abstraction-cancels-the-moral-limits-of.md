## Verbatim key claims

### C-1 (p3, Abstract) — Diagnosis: implicit moral inversion (LOAD-BEARING)
> "Modern political economy rests on an implicit moral inversion: money, originally a means of exchange, has become the governing standard of value. This paper traces that inversion to a pivotal but underexamined move in early liberal property theory — the treatment of money as a morally exceptional object."

### C-2 (p3, Abstract) — Locke's three provisos as life-protective baseline
> "Reconstructing the original life-grounded constraints on private property articulated by **John Locke**, the analysis shows how labor, sufficiency, and non-waste once functioned as intrinsic moral limits. The introduction of money, conceived as non-perishable and value-neutral, quietly bypassed these limits in practice without refuting them in principle."

### C-3 (p3, Abstract) — Naming the move + reversing the value order (CENTRAL THESIS)
> "The paper names this bypass the *money exception* and demonstrates how it reverses the value order of society: life ceases to govern property, and property — measured monetarily — comes to govern life. Drawing on **John McMurtry**'s Life-Value Onto-Axiology, the paper distinguishes between life-sequence and money-sequence systems and explains why money-governed systems reliably generate ecological degradation, public health failure, and social insecurity while appearing economically successful."

### C-4 (p3, Abstract) — Non-rejection of markets stance
> "Rather than rejecting markets or private property, the paper argues for restoring the life-grounded conditions that once made them morally intelligible. It reframes contemporary crises as a correctable design flaw in the moral architecture of political economy and offers a coherent basis for re-embedding money, markets, and property within the requirements of life itself."

### C-5 (p3, Executive Summary) — Locke's life-protective conditions (verbatim 3-condition restatement)
> "Early liberal theories of property did not defend unlimited accumulation. On the contrary, they justified private ownership only under strict life-protective conditions. Property was legitimate only when grounded in labor, constrained by sufficiency for others, and limited by non-waste. These conditions ensured that private appropriation remained compatible with shared survival in a finite world."

### C-6 (p3, Exec Summary) — The decisive shift (LOAD-BEARING)
> "The decisive shift occurred with the introduction of money. Treated as non-perishable, morally neutral, and universally exchangeable, money was exempted from the very constraints that governed material goods. This exemption — the money exception — did not openly reject life-based limits. It bypassed them. Once accumulation took monetary rather than material form, the original moral constraints ceased to operate in practice."

### C-7 (p3, Exec Summary) — Cascading consequences
> "The consequences of this shift are profound. Money becomes the ultimate standard of value, while life — human labor, ecosystems, social systems — is reduced to an input. Markets are elevated from tools to moral arbiters. States increasingly define their role as protectors of monetary claims rather than guarantors of life conditions. Public health, environmental protection, and social care are reframed as costs rather than foundations."

### C-8 (p3, Exec Summary) — Life-sequence vs money-sequence distinction (LOAD-BEARING — composes with Spore ADR-0048 substitution-trap)
> "Using Life-Value Onto-Axiology, the paper clarifies why these outcomes are not anomalies but predictable results of a money-governed value system. It distinguishes between a **life-sequence**, in which money serves life, and a **money-sequence**, in which life serves money. Modern crises emerge when institutions built on the latter are expected to deliver the outcomes of the former."

### C-9 (p3, Exec Summary) — Reform thesis (life as governing standard, not abolition)
> "The paper concludes that meaningful reform does not require abolishing markets, property, or money. It requires restoring life as the governing standard by which they are judged. When property and exchange are once again conditional on sustaining life capacities, ethical limits reappear as **intrinsic design constraints rather than external impositions**."

### C-10 (p4-5, §1 Introduction) — Property as architecture-of-life
> "Property determines who may use land, water, housing, tools, knowledge, and labor — and under what conditions. It silently structures the economy long before questions of taxation, redistribution, or welfare arise. If the rules of property are misaligned with the requirements of life, no amount of policy fine-tuning downstream can fully correct the damage. The system will continue to produce outcomes that are internally 'rational' yet externally destructive."

### C-11 (p5, §1) — "What is property for?" reframe
> "Contemporary debates tend to frame property conflicts as a struggle between markets and states, or between individual freedom and collective control. These framings obscure a more fundamental question: **What is property for?** Is it an unconditional right to accumulate claims, or is it a conditional institution meant to serve human and ecological flourishing? The answer to this question determines whether economic systems provision life — or quietly erode it."

### C-12 (p9, §4 end) — The transformation does not announce itself (LOAD-BEARING)
> "What makes this transformation especially powerful is that it does not announce itself as a moral revolution. There is no explicit argument that life-based constraints are wrong or obsolete. Instead, they are quietly bypassed. Money is treated as an exception to the rules rather than as a new value standard — yet over time, the exception becomes the norm."

### C-13 (p9, §4 end) — Abstraction tendency
> "This is not a historical accident or a personal failing on Locke's part. It reflects a deeper tendency within abstraction itself. When value is represented symbolically, its connection to concrete life conditions becomes easier to ignore. Moral limits tied to perishability, sufficiency, and use dissolve when value is redefined as something that can be stored indefinitely and exchanged universally."

### C-14 (p9, §5 Money Exception Explained) — The structure of the bypass
> "At this point, the structure of the shift becomes visible. What has occurred is not the rejection of moral limits on property, but their suspension through a special case. Money is treated as an object that stands outside the ethical rules governing material goods. This special status is what later philosophers describe as the *money exception*."

### C-15 (p10, §5) — Exception-becomes-norm mechanism (LOAD-BEARING for Spore F6 failure-modes ADR-0075 §F6.8 meta-pattern)
> "An exception, in moral and legal systems, is never neutral. It marks a boundary where ordinary rules no longer apply. When an exception is narrow and temporary, it may be harmless. When it is permanent and foundational, it quietly rewrites the entire system. Money functions in precisely this way. It is declared exempt from spoilage, exempt from sufficiency constraints, and exempt from direct accountability to life-need. Yet because it mediates access to all material goods, this exemption propagates throughout the entire economy."

### C-16 (p10, §5) — Invisibility as power-source
> "The power of the money exception lies in its invisibility. Money is treated as a mere instrument of exchange rather than as a bearer of moral consequence. Because it does not rot, it appears to violate no one directly. Because it is abstract, it conceals the material and biological processes it commands. As a result, accumulation in monetary form comes to appear ethically weightless, even when it systematically deprives others of land, labor, housing, food, or healthcare."

### C-17 (p10, §5) — The critical inversion (HIGHEST-LEVERAGE FRAMING)
> "This is the critical inversion. In Locke's original framework, life set the standard, and property was judged by its effects on shared survival. Under the money exception, money sets the standard, and life is judged by its contribution to monetary growth. Labor becomes a cost to be minimized. Nature becomes a resource stock. Social systems become 'inefficiencies' unless they generate financial returns."

### C-18 (p10, §5) — Inversion-not-from-malice
> "Importantly, this inversion does not depend on malice, conspiracy, or moral corruption. It arises from the internal logic of treating money as value-neutral. Once money is assumed to be morally inert, any action that increases monetary accumulation can be justified, regardless of its life consequences. Harm is reframed as an externality. Deprivation is recast as market outcome. Systemic damage becomes statistically invisible until it reaches crisis proportions."

### C-19 (p10, §5) — McMurtry's life-sequence vs money-sequence (LOAD-BEARING canonical claim)
> "It is here that the analysis of **John McMurtry** becomes decisive. McMurtry identifies two opposed value sequences operating beneath modern political economy. In the **life-sequence**, goods, institutions, and practices are judged by whether they enable and sustain life capacities. In the **money-sequence**, life capacities are treated as inputs for generating monetary returns. The money exception is the mechanism that allows the money-sequence to dominate while appearing morally neutral."

### C-20 (p10, §5) — System cannot recognize own failure modes (LOAD-BEARING — composes with #4 C-6)
> "Once this exception is normalized, the system no longer recognizes its own failure modes. Ecological collapse is interpreted as a pricing problem. Public health breakdown becomes a budgetary issue. Mass insecurity is explained as lack of competitiveness. The system continues to function according to its internal metrics even as the life systems on which it depends are degraded."

### C-21 (p11, §6 Life-Bound→Money-Bound) — Structural inversion (LOAD-BEARING)
> "In the original life-bound framework, property functioned as a means. Its justification depended on outcomes: nourishment, shelter, productive participation, and continuity of shared life. The ethical question was always external to property itself — *What does this arrangement do to living beings and the systems that support them?* Accumulation beyond use raised moral alarms because it signaled a break between ownership and life function. Under money-bound value, that relationship is reversed. Money accumulation becomes the primary indicator of success, and life outcomes are treated as secondary effects. Property no longer needs to demonstrate that it serves life; life must demonstrate that it serves property's monetary expansion. **The means becomes the end.**"

### C-22 (p11, §6) — Diagnostic question displacement
> "Social institutions follow the same pattern. Public health, education, housing, and care are reinterpreted as expenditures rather than as life-preserving infrastructures. If they cannot be justified in monetary terms — return on investment, cost–benefit ratios, fiscal sustainability — they are deemed inefficient. The question *'Does this keep people alive and capable?'* is displaced by *'Can this be afforded?'* — with affordability defined in monetary, not life, terms."
