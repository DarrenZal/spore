## Verbatim key claims (14 claims; selective-high-fidelity)

### C-1 (p8, Abstract — LOAD-BEARING) — Civilizational paradox + 3-layer architecture
> "Modern civilization faces an increasing divergence between the ecological systems that sustain life and the institutional and cultural frameworks through which societies organize themselves. While technological and economic capacity have expanded rapidly, ecological degradation, institutional fragility, and cultural polarization suggest that many societies are drifting toward systemic instability. This paper introduces the **Violence–Viability Architecture, an integrative framework that conceptualizes civilization as a three-layer system composed of the life-ground, institutional governance structures, and cultural narratives**."

### C-2 (p8, Abstract — LOAD-BEARING) — Viability corridor + reflexive civilization
> "The paper further proposes the concept of a **civilizational viability corridor, defined by the interaction between ecological integrity, institutional capacity, and cultural coherence**. By identifying early warning indicators and policy diagnostic tools, the framework provides a practical approach for evaluating whether governance systems strengthen or undermine the conditions required for long-term societal stability. The analysis concludes by exploring the possibility of a transition toward **reflexive civilization, in which societies consciously monitor and manage the ecological and institutional systems upon which their survival depends.**"

### C-3 (p11, Introduction — LOAD-BEARING) — Central diagnostic question
> "Why do societies repeatedly drift toward conflict and ecological degradation **even when the long-term consequences of such trajectories are widely understood?**"

### C-4 (p12, Introduction — extends Wave-5 #2 C-6) — Three-layer interdependence (replicated)
> "These three layers are deeply interconnected. Ecological disruptions can destabilize institutions. Institutional failures can intensify social grievances. **Cultural narratives shape how populations interpret crises and determine whether societies respond through cooperation or conflict.**"

### C-5 (p36, §5.2 Polyvagal Model — LOAD-BEARING; DISTINCTIVE) — Three-mode autonomic regulation
> "**Polyvagal theory**, proposed by neuroscientist Stephen Porges... According to this model, the autonomic nervous system regulates behavior through three broad modes:
> 1. **Social engagement (ventral vagal regulation)** — When individuals perceive safety, the nervous system supports calm attention, communication, and cooperation.
> 2. **Mobilization (sympathetic activation)** — When threats are perceived, the body prepares for action through heightened arousal and defensive mobilization.
> 3. **Immobilization (dorsal vagal shutdown)** — Under extreme threat, the nervous system may shift toward withdrawal or collapse responses."

### C-6 (p37, §5.3 — LOAD-BEARING) — Safety enables HDT cognitive complexity
> "When individuals experience safety and stability, physiological regulation allows for greater cognitive flexibility and social openness. Under these conditions, the brain can devote resources to complex reasoning, long-term planning, and cooperative interaction... At the societal level, these conditions support the development of cultural frameworks that emphasize: pluralism, negotiation, scientific inquiry, and collaborative problem-solving. **Such orientations align with the integrative cultural mode described earlier as holism, dialectics, and transcendence (HDT).**"

### C-7 (p37, §5.4 — LOAD-BEARING) — Threat-narrative-simplification (CMT/DMA/RP biological substrate)
> "When individuals or communities perceive threat, the nervous system shifts toward defensive modes of regulation. Mobilization responses heighten attention to potential dangers and increase sensitivity to signals associated with group identity and competition. Under such conditions, cognitive processes often simplify complex realities into clearer categories of friend and enemy. Narratives emphasizing moral certainty, group loyalty, and existential struggle become psychologically compelling. These patterns resemble the cultural attractors described in Section III: **Chosenness–Myth–Trauma (CMT)** reinforces group identity and historical grievance. **Dualism–Manichaeism–Armageddon (DMA)** frames conflict in absolute moral terms. **Repression–Projection (RP)** externalizes internal tensions onto perceived adversaries. These narrative structures may therefore represent cultural expressions of deeper physiological responses to perceived insecurity."

### C-8 (p38, §5.5 — LOAD-BEARING; DISTINCTIVE) — Collective nervous systems
> "Although nervous systems operate at the level of individual organisms, **human societies exhibit forms of collective emotional regulation**. Shared media environments, public discourse, political rhetoric, and cultural rituals can synchronize emotional responses across large populations. Events perceived as threatening — economic crises, environmental disasters, or geopolitical conflicts — can trigger widespread shifts in public mood... **In this sense, cultural dynamics may reflect the aggregate regulatory states of millions of interacting nervous systems.**"

### C-9 (p38, §5.6 — LOAD-BEARING) — Institutions-as-collective-stress-regulators
> "By ensuring access to basic life necessities, these institutions reduce chronic stress and insecurity. When such systems function effectively, they create conditions under which cooperative cognition and dialogue become more likely. Conversely, when institutions fail to provide basic security — when access to water, healthcare, food, or economic stability becomes uncertain — populations may experience chronic stress... **From this perspective, institutions can be understood as societal mechanisms for regulating collective stress.**"

### C-10 (p39, §5.7) — Beyond narrative-alone intervention
> "If societies wish to reduce the likelihood of conflict, addressing cultural narratives alone may be insufficient. Efforts to promote cooperation must also consider the **material and institutional conditions that influence collective perceptions of safety or threat**. Strengthening the life-ground and the institutions that sustain it may therefore play a crucial role in shaping the cultural dynamics of societies."
