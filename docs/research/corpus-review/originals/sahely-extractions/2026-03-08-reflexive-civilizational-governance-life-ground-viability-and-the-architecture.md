## Verbatim key claims (14 claims; selective-high-fidelity)

### C-1 (p4, Epigraph #3 — DISTINCTIVE; Bateson citation NEW for cluster) — Bateson framing
> "The major problems of the world are the result of the difference between how nature works and the way people think." — Gregory Bateson

### C-2 (p5, Preface — LOAD-BEARING) — Trilogy unification thesis
> "The Violence–Viability trilogy attempts to bridge these perspectives by developing a unified framework for understanding civilizational stability. The framework begins with a simple proposition: **the ultimate foundation of any civilization is the life-ground — the ecological systems that provide the material conditions necessary for human survival.** Institutions, economies, and cultural traditions all emerge within this broader ecological context."

### C-3 (p5-6, Preface — LOAD-BEARING) — Reflexive governance distinctive contribution of Paper III
> "The present volume extends this analysis by examining how civilizations may develop **reflexive governance systems capable of monitoring and regulating their own systemic dynamics**. Such systems represent an evolutionary step in the organization of human societies — one in which institutions, knowledge systems, and cultural narratives align around the shared task of sustaining the ecological foundations of civilization."

### C-4 (p7-8, Trilogy Overview — LOAD-BEARING; CLUSTER-DEFINITIVE) — Trilogy structure (3 papers enumerated)
> "Paper I — **The Violence–Viability Architecture**: integrates Galtung's structural/cultural violence with McMurtry's LVOA. Within this architecture, civilizations are understood as layered systems composed of: (1) the life-ground (ecological foundations), (2) **infrastructure systems** that transform ecological resources, (3) institutions that coordinate resource management, (4) cultural narratives that shape collective meaning. When these layers remain aligned with the life-ground, societies maintain stability.
> Paper II — **Systemic Drift and Civilizational Instability**: examines the dynamics through which civilizations move away from stability. Key concepts: **systemic drift, temporal elasticity, narrative attractors, threshold dynamics**.
> Paper III — **Reflexive Civilizational Governance**: explores regulatory mechanisms through which societies may maintain alignment with the life-ground. Introduces: **the epistemic commons, the reflexive governance cycle, civilizational stability landscapes**."

### C-5 (p8, Trilogy Overview — Central Question) — Unified trilogy question
> "Can human civilization develop institutions capable of sustaining life within the ecological boundaries of the Earth system while preserving the conditions that allow human communities to flourish?"

### C-6 (p9, Abstract — LOAD-BEARING; DISTINCTIVE) — Five-layer architecture (NEW)
> "Building upon the Violence–Viability Architecture developed in earlier work, this paper introduces the concept of reflexive civilizational governance. The framework integrates **five interacting layers of civilizational organization: the life-ground, infrastructure systems, institutional governance, the epistemic commons, and cultural narratives**. Within this architecture, systemic instability emerges when signals from ecological and social systems fail to propagate effectively through knowledge institutions and governance structures, allowing pressures to accumulate until critical thresholds are crossed."

### C-7 (p9, Abstract) — Central argument of Paper III
> "The central argument is that long-term civilizational stability depends on the emergence of **reflexive institutions capable of continuously monitoring, interpreting, and responding to changes in the life-ground**. Civilizations that develop such capacities can navigate systemic shocks and ecological constraints while sustaining human flourishing. Those that fail to do so risk entering reinforcing cycles of structural violence, institutional capture, and ecological overshoot."

### C-8 (p10, Exec Summary — LOAD-BEARING; DISTINCTIVE) — Epistemic commons concept
> "It introduces the concept of the **epistemic commons, the network of scientific institutions, education systems, media infrastructures, and information flows through which societies perceive and interpret reality. These systems function as the cognitive infrastructure of civilization.** When the epistemic commons functions effectively, societies can detect ecological stress, evaluate evidence, and adapt policies accordingly. When it becomes fragmented or captured by narrow interests, societies may lose the ability to recognize systemic risks until crises become unavoidable."

### C-9 (p10, Exec Summary) — Temporal elasticity (carried from Paper II)
> "The paper incorporates a temporal dimension through the concept of **temporal elasticity**. Modern societies frequently maintain apparent stability by borrowing resilience from the future through mechanisms such as ecological depletion, infrastructure neglect, financial debt, and social inequality. These processes allow systemic pressures to accumulate gradually while masking underlying fragility. As a result, civilizational collapse often appears sudden even though instability has been developing for decades."

### C-10 (p10-11, Exec Summary — LOAD-BEARING; DISTINCTIVE) — Three analytical tools
> "To analyze these dynamics, the paper introduces several conceptual tools:
> • a **five-layer architecture of civilizational viability** integrating ecological, infrastructural, institutional, epistemic, and cultural systems
> • a **civilizational phase space** describing how societies move across stability landscapes under conditions of stress
> • a **reflexive governance loop** linking ecological monitoring, scientific knowledge, public discourse, institutional decision-making, and social feedback"

### C-11 (p11, Exec Summary — LOAD-BEARING) — Conclusion: reflexive civilizational stability
> "**The central conclusion is that the long-term stability of civilization depends upon the emergence of reflexive governance systems capable of observing and correcting their own systemic dynamics.** Such systems require strong scientific institutions, transparent information ecosystems, adaptive governance structures, and cultural narratives that recognize the interdependence between human societies and the ecological systems upon which they depend."

### C-12 (p13, Introduction — LOAD-BEARING; the knowing-but-not-doing paradox) — Core paradox restated
> "Human societies possess an unprecedented capacity to observe and analyze the systems upon which they depend... Yet despite this growing knowledge, many societies struggle to translate scientific understanding into effective collective action. **This paradox raises a fundamental question about the nature of civilizational stability. Why do societies frequently fail to respond effectively to systemic risks even when those risks are widely recognized?**"

### C-13 (TOC §1 + §7 + Appendix A4 — LOAD-BEARING substrate primitive) — Epistemic commons as nervous-system metaphor
