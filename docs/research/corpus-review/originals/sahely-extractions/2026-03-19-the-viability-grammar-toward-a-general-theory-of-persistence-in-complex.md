## Verbatim key claims (16 claims; selective-high-fidelity)

### C-1 (p7, Abstract — LOAD-BEARING) — Unification thesis across 5 traditions
> "Research on this problem has emerged across several intellectual traditions, including **cybernetics, resilience ecology, viability theory, predictive processing, and institutional governance studies**. However, these traditions have largely evolved in parallel, resulting in fragmented conceptual frameworks for analyzing adaptive persistence. This paper proposes a unifying framework — the viability grammar — that identifies **seven structural elements governing the persistence of complex adaptive systems: constraints, margins, optionality, disturbances, perception, regulation, and system state**."

### C-2 (p7, Abstract — LOAD-BEARING; DISTINCTIVE) — Triadic Generative Hypothesis
> "Building on this structure, the paper advances a **triadic generative hypothesis suggesting that the viability grammar may emerge from the interaction of three fundamental system dimensions: constraints, perception, and regulation**. Disturbances act as forcing fields that perturb system trajectories, while margins and optionality arise from the relationship between system state and constraint geometry."

### C-3 (p7, Abstract) — Cross-scale recurrence claim
> "Evidence from physical, biological, ecological, and institutional systems suggests that **the same structural architecture recurs across multiple levels of organization**. The viability grammar therefore offers a common conceptual language for analyzing resilience, adaptation, and system collapse across domains."

### C-4 (p9, Exec Summary) — Seven core elements enumerated
> "The framework identifies seven core elements that determine whether system trajectories remain within viable regions of state space:
> • **Constraints** – the limits defining viable system states
> • **Margins** – the distance between the system state and constraint boundaries
> • **Optionality** – the range of viable future trajectories available to the system
> • **Disturbances** – perturbations affecting system dynamics
> • **Perception** – mechanisms through which systems detect or estimate conditions
> • **Regulation** – actions capable of steering system trajectories
> • **System State / Trajectory** – the realized path of the system through time"

### C-5 (p26, §5 opening — LOAD-BEARING; DISTINCTIVE) — Triadic generator (C, P, R)
> "The relational structure described in the previous section suggests that **the seven elements of the viability grammar may not all be independent primitives. Instead, the pattern of triadic relations indicates that the grammar may emerge from a smaller set of fundamental dimensions governing system persistence.** This observation motivates what we refer to as the **triadic generative hypothesis**. According to this hypothesis, the viability grammar arises from the interaction of three primary system dimensions: **(C, P, R)** where C represents constraints, the structural limits imposed by reality; P represents perception, the mechanisms through which systems estimate relevant conditions; R represents regulation, the actions available to influence system dynamics."

### C-6 (p26, §5 — LOAD-BEARING) — Three fundamental requirements for adaptive system
> "These three elements correspond to **three fundamental requirements faced by any adaptive system: 1. The system must operate within real constraints. 2. The system must detect or estimate relevant environmental conditions. 3. The system must possess mechanisms capable of regulating its behavior.** Together these dimensions define the minimal architecture required for viable system dynamics."

### C-7 (p26, §5.1 — LOAD-BEARING) — Constraint as ontological foundation
> "Constraints determine the region of state space within which system trajectories remain viable. Without constraints, viability would have no meaning, as there would be no boundaries separating viable and nonviable states. **Constraints therefore represent the ontological foundation of the viability grammar.**"

### C-8 (p27, §5.2 — LOAD-BEARING) — Perception as epistemic dimension
> "Perception provides the system with estimates of relevant variables that influence regulatory decisions. Without perception, regulatory actions would occur blindly, greatly increasing the risk of constraint violations. **Perception therefore represents the epistemic dimension of the viability grammar.**"

### C-9 (p27, §5.3 — LOAD-BEARING) — Regulation as action dimension
> "Effective regulation requires the capacity to alter system trajectories in ways that maintain viability under disturbance. **Regulation therefore represents the action dimension of the viability grammar.**"

### C-10 (p28, §5.4 — LOAD-BEARING; formal) — Disturbance as forcing field D(t)
> "We therefore introduce a **disturbance field D(t) representing external or internal perturbations affecting system dynamics**. Disturbances may include environmental fluctuations, resource shocks, technological disruptions, or internal system instabilities. Within the triadic framework, **disturbances act as forcing factors that challenge system viability.**"

### C-11 (p28, §5.5 — LOAD-BEARING; formal) — State as derived from (C, P, R, D)
> "Given the triadic generator and disturbance field, system state evolves according to the interaction of constraints, perception, and regulatory action under disturbance. We can express this relationship conceptually as **X = h(C, P, R, D; t)** where X represents the system's realized trajectory through time. **State is therefore not a primitive element but rather the historical outcome of the interaction among the generating dimensions.**"

### C-12 (p28-29, §5.6 — LOAD-BEARING; formal) — Margins emerge from state × constraint
> "If constraint conditions are expressed as **C_j(x, t) ≤ 0, j = 1, …, k** then the margin relative to each constraint can be defined as **m_j(x, t) = −C_j(x, t)**. For states within the viability domain, m_j(x, t) ≥ 0, and the magnitude of m_j represents the distance from the constraint boundary. The system's effective margin corresponds to the smallest margin across all constraints: **M(x, t) = min_j m_j(x, t)**. Thus, the effective margin represents the minimum distance between the current system state and any constraint boundary. **Margins therefore represent a derived property emerging from the relationship between system [state and constraints]**."
