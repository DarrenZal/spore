- [html-section:CARTA — Domestication and Human Evolution] "The domestication of other species has played an undeniably central role in the evolution of modern humans, and in our planetary dominance and success."

- [html-section:CARTA — Domestication and Human Evolution] "Recently, a convergence of views has led to the notion that the study of animal domestication may tell us something not only about our relationship with domesticated species since perhaps at least the Pleistocene, but also about our own evolution as a species in the more distant past."

- [html-section:Robert Franciscus — Craniofacial Feminization] "He then describes a promising model for the advent of facial diminution, which suggests that anatomically modern humans represent a ‘self-domesticated’ species where selection for increased social tolerance led to growth and developmental alterations producing craniofacial “feminization,” which itself results in a phenotypic signal of reduced aggressiveness."

- [html-section:Terrence Deacon — The Domesticated Brain] "In this talk Terrence Deacon (UC Berkeley) describes how the signature pattern of specific brain structure changes can provide evidence to distinguish between the processes associated with domestication."

- [html-section:Tecumseh Fitch — Domestication Syndrome and Neural Crest Cells] "Tecumseh Fitch (Univ of Vienna) hypothesizes that the selection for tameness during early stages of domestication led to delayed maturation and reduced output of the adrenal component of the “fight or flight” response, via reduced neural crest input."

- [html-section:Kazuo Okanoya — Vocal Behavior in Finches] "He shows that evolution of song complexity involves not only factors related to sexual selection and species identification, but also to socio-emotional factors due to domestication. He then speculates that language evolution in humans might also be based on sexual selection and self-domestication."

- [html-section:Richard Wrangham — Did Homo Sapiens Self-Domesticate?] "In this talk Richard Wrangham (Harvard Univ) puts forth the theory that Homo sapiens are, in fact, a self-domesticated species. He defines “self-domestication” as the evolution of a reduced propensity for reactive aggression (compared to an immediate ancestor), without the active involvement of another species."

- [html-section:Richard Wrangham — Did Homo Sapiens Self-Domesticate?] "He then shows that communal sanctions practiced by hunter-gatherers, which depend on proactive aggression, provide a leading candidate mechanism selecting against high levels of reactive aggression."

- [html-section:Richard Wrangham — Did Homo Sapiens Self-Domesticate?] "He therefore proposes that human self-domestication is an ironic consequence of a particularly well-developed facility for proactive aggression, and concludes that humans did indeed self-domesticate, providing a critical underpinning for inter-individual tolerance and cooperation."

- [html-section:Further Resources — Wilkins/Wrangham/Fitch 2014 abstract] "Here, we propose that the domestication syndrome results predominantly from mild neural crest cell deficits during embryonic development."

- [html-section:Further Resources — Theofanopoulou et al. 2017 abstract] "The results obtained suggest that (paleo-)genomic data can be exploited to complement the fossil record and support the idea of self-domestication in Homo sapiens, a process that likely intensified as our species populated its niche."
