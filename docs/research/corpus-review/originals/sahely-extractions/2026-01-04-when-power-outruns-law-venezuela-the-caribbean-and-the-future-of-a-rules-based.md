## Verbatim key claims (8 claims; selective depth for non-anchor)

### C-1 (p4, Author's Foreword) — Medical-diagnostic framing of system failure (LOAD-BEARING; bridges medicine to political coordination grammar)
> "In medicine, we are taught to distinguish symptoms from causes, urgency from emergency, and intervention from harm. Acting too late can cost lives. Acting too fast, without evidence or proportion, can do the same. The same logic applies to societies. When power overrides process in the name of necessity, the immediate outcome may feel decisive, but the long-term damage often goes unseen until it is too late to reverse."

### C-2 (p4, Author's Foreword) — International law as protective infrastructure
> "Living and working in the Caribbean sharpens this awareness. Small states do not have the luxury of abstraction when international norms weaken. We experience disruption not as theory, but as grounded flights, disrupted supply chains, strained hospitals, sudden migration pressures, and communities caught between forces they did not create. For us, international law is not an academic discipline; it is **protective infrastructure**."

### C-3 (p7, §1) — Law fails by drift not collapse (LOAD-BEARING; failure-mode-class match to Spore F6.4 scale-transition + F6.1 representation failure-modes)
> "What we are witnessing now is not the sudden collapse of that order, but something subtler and more dangerous: the gradual normalization of exceptions. Actions that once required extraordinary justification are increasingly framed as routine. Legal boundaries are not openly rejected; instead, they are reinterpreted, stretched, or bypassed in ways that preserve the language of law while hollowing out its function.
> 
> This matters because law does not fail all at once. It fails by drift."

### C-4 (p17, §6.1) — Law as restraint, not decoration; introduction of friction-as-feature
> "Law was never meant to eliminate power. It was meant to **shape** it. Law introduces friction — procedures, thresholds, justifications — that slow decisions down and force reasons into the open. This friction is not a flaw; it is the point. Without it, decisions default to convenience, fear, or advantage.
> 
> When power outruns law, law does not disappear. It remains present as language, as ritual, as paperwork. But it ceases to function as a restraint. Instead, it becomes a post-hoc justification — a way of explaining what has already been done rather than governing what may be done.
> 
> At that moment, legality shifts from a guide to a gloss."

### C-5 (p17, §6.3) — Semantic drift as failure mode (resonates with Spore ADR-0033 dual-axis pattern-recursion + scope-conditioning discipline)
> "One of the most insidious effects of power outrunning law is the corruption of language. Terms that once carried shared meaning begin to stretch and blur.
> 
> 'Self-defense' no longer refers strictly to response against armed attack; it expands to include prevention, deterrence, and even punishment. 'Terrorism' shifts from a description of methods to a label for enemies. 'Justice' becomes synonymous with success.
> 
> This semantic drift matters because law depends on shared definitions. When words lose stable meaning, argument becomes impossible. Disagreement is no longer about interpretation; it is about allegiance. At that point, law cannot mediate conflict — it can only rationalize it."

### C-6 (p17, §6.4) — Order without legitimacy is brittle (resonates with Spore ADR-0042 structural-legitimacy + F8 external-validation-loop)
> "Power can create order without law. History is full of such examples. The problem is that order without legitimacy is never self-sustaining. It relies on continuous enforcement, escalating coercion, and constant vigilance against dissent.
> 
> Legitimacy, by contrast, allows order to reproduce itself with less force. People comply not only because they must, but because the system makes sense to them. When legitimacy erodes, enforcement costs rise. Resistance becomes more likely. Neutrality becomes harder to maintain."

### C-7 (p17, §7) — Small states as canaries / early indicator (resonates with Spore F1 sensor-oracle-governance + F8 external-validation-loop)
> "When power begins to outrun law, its earliest effects are rarely felt where decisions are made. They are felt where consequences land. Small states occupy that downstream position in the international system. They experience first what others will experience later."

### C-8 (p20, §8.4) — Truth as restraint; evidence as de-escalation infrastructure (load-bearing; viability-grammar bridge)
> "The discipline of truth does not guarantee justice. It does not prevent conflict. But it does impose restraint. It forces decision-makers to confront uncertainty rather than erase it. It creates space for alternatives to force — diplomacy, mediation, containment, or patience."
