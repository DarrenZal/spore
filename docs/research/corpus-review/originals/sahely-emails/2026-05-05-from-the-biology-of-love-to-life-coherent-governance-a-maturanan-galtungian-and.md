Executive Summary
The previous companion papers developed a biological arc from autopoiesis to living coherence, an affective arc from emotioning to viable affective coupling, and an evolutionary arc from natural drift to evolutionary living coherence. The ethical-political arc begins from the same premise: living systems cannot be understood apart from the relations that conserve or disable their viability. The affective white paper already argued that institutions and policies shaping exclusion, insecurity, domination, housing precarity, ecological degradation, or humiliation are not merely “social” conditions but can shape affective biology and disease risk.

Maturana’s biology of love provides the biological-ethical ground. Love names the domain of relational behaviors in which the other arises as a legitimate other in coexistence. This does not make politics sentimental. It makes social life biological: fear, domination, exclusion, humiliation, and coercion narrow the domains of action available to persons and communities, while recognition, dignity, and reciprocity expand them.

Galtung provides the diagnostic grammar of harm. In “Violence, Peace, and Peace Research,” he introduced structural violence as harm built into social structures rather than only direct physical injury. In “Cultural Violence,” he later defined cultural violence as any aspect of culture that can be used to legitimize direct or structural violence (Galtung, 1969, 1990).

McMurtry provides the evaluative criterion. His life-value analysis argues that social systems must be judged by whether they enable or disable universal life goods, life necessities, and civil commons infrastructures. In this view, the common interest is grounded not in price, profit, preference, or corporate right, but in the conditions required for life-capacity to be maintained and developed (McMurtry, 2011).

Ostrom provides a governance grammar. Her work on common-pool resources challenged the assumption that shared resources must be governed only by privatization or centralized command, showing that communities can self-organize durable commons institutions under appropriate conditions (Ostrom, 1990, 2009).

The central thesis is:

Life-coherent governance is the non-forcing coordination of social, ecological, economic, and institutional conditions so that living beings can conserve and expand their capacities for life, participation, repair, and coexistence.
