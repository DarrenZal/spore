## 2. C-claims (verbatim, from the extraction record)

All quotes are **Geoff Mulgan's words** (_Big Mind_, pp. 70–75), reposted by Sahely. `[html-section:]` anchors per the extraction record.

- **C1** [html-section:Learning Loops] "Our default is to depend on what we already know and change only incrementally, in small adjacent steps. This is the logic of evolution, in which big changes tend to be the consequence of many small changes rather than giant leaps."

- **C2** [html-section:Learning Loops] "The mark of any intelligent creature, institution, or system is that it is able to learn. It may make mistakes, but it won't generally repeat them. That requires an ability to organize intelligence into a series of loops, which have a logical and hierarchical relationship to each other."

- **C3** [html-section:First-loop learning] "First-loop learning is what we recognize as everyday thought. It involves the application of thinking methods to definable questions… We begin with models of how the world works as well as models of thinking, and then we gather data about the external and internal worlds, based on categories. Then we act and observe when the world does or does not respond as expected, and adjust our actions and the details of our models in response to the data."

- **C4** [html-section:First-loop learning] "What's more remarkable is how many institutions lack even basic learning loops of this kind, and so continue to make unnecessary mistakes, assume facts that aren't true, and deny the obvious."

- **C5** [html-section:Second-loop learning] "Second-loop learning becomes relevant when the models no longer work or there are too many surprises. It may be necessary to generate new categories because the old ones don't work… and it may be necessary to generate a new model… This second loop also involves the ability to reflect on goals and means."

- **C6** [html-section:Second-loop learning] "This is the well-known trade-off between exploitation and exploration. Exploitation of what we already know is predictable and usually sensible. But if we never explore, we risk stagnation or at least missing out on new opportunities."

- **C7** [html-section:Third-loop learning] "Third-loop learning involves the ability to reflect on and change how we think — our underlying ontologies, epistemologies, and types of logic… We recognize third-loop learning to have happened when a radically new way of thinking has become normal, with its own tools and methods, and its own view of what is and what matters."

- **C8** [html-section:Third-loop learning] "Most fundamental social change also involves just such third-loop learning — not just doing new things to others. This is the implication of Audre Lorde's famous comment that we cannot use the master's tools to dismantle the master's house."

- **C9** [html-section:body] "Indeed, the psychological growth of any individual involves passing through all three loops repeatedly, as the individual learns about the world, and also reevaluates their place in the world and how to conceive of it."

- **C10** [html-section:body] "This approach also helps to make sense of how all practical intelligence strikes a balance between inclusion and exclusion. Some types of data and patterns are attended to; others are ignored. This selectivity applies to everything from natural language to sensors. More is not always better… Practical intelligence has to select."

- **C11** [html-section:body] "In stable environments, the first-loop reasoners will tend to do best. In unstable ones, where any item of knowledge has a half-life of decay, the groups with more capacity to reimagine their categories and thinking modes may adapt better… But it is inherently impossible to know what the best balance is except in retrospect."

- **C12** [html-section:footnote 1] "This framework draws on yet extends the famous distinction made by Argyris and Schon between single-loop learning, which learns from new facts, but doesn't question the goal or logic being followed, and double-loop learning that asks the bigger questions."

## 4. Substrate-resonance map (descriptive)

Read against the **resolved** post-Bundle-α canon (14 foundation docs; 9 primitives; 4 cross-cutting doctrines; 2 modes; 2 properties; concepts-yaml v24; 40 canon-decisions through ADR-0090). All observations descriptive; nothing admitted. **All claims are Mulgan's; Sahely's contribution is curation only.**

### 4.1 Three loops ↔ Spore's three rule-levels — the load-bearing resonance

Mulgan's three nested loops (C2/C3/C5/C7) and Spore's Ostrom 3-level rule-stack (`operational-rule` / `collective-choice-rule` / `constitutional-rule`; ADR-0046 field-rule-level-stratification) share a **recursive-not-strictly-hierarchical** structure where the most common activity sits at the lowest level and higher-level revision is rarer and costlier:

- **First-loop** (apply thinking methods to definable questions; adjust model details to data) ↔ **operational rules** (day-to-day coordination within the field).
- **Second-loop** (generate new categories/models when the old ones fail; reflect on goals and means) ↔ **collective-choice rules** (how operational rules are made, modified, enforced).
- **Third-loop** (change underlying ontologies/epistemologies/logics) ↔ **constitutional rules** (who is eligible to participate at the collective-choice level / the deepest "view of what is and what matters").

This is a **resonance, not an identity**: Mulgan's axis is *cognitive learning depth* (how deep a revision is); Spore's axis is *rule-making authority level* (which action-situation a rule governs). The two coincide on the structural insight that **revision is stratified, recursive, and increasingly rare/expensive as you go deeper** — but a cognitive-learning frame is not a coordination-rule frame, and this note does not propose collapsing them. Mulgan's own footnote 1 (C12) grounds the framework in Argyris & Schön single/double-loop, the same organizational-learning lineage Ostrom's rule-stratification is adjacent to.

### 4.2 Conservation-through-change ↔ `rule-in-use` (ADR-0046)

C1's "depend on what we already know and change only incrementally" and C9's "passing through all three loops repeatedly" describe a system that conserves its working model until error/surprise forces revision. This parallels Spore's `rule-in-use` discipline (ADR-0046): the **working rule participants actually reference** is authoritative; formal written rules project that practice; revision is assessed against rules-in-use, not rules-in-form. The cognitive analogue is exact — the working mental model is load-bearing until the world stops responding as expected (C3), at which point second-loop revision generates a new model.

### 4.3 Frame-change and generativity ↔ enactive bringing-forth / `expressive-power` / `constructed-power`

C5/C7's "generate new categories" and "change underlying ontologies, epistemologies, and types of logic" is the **bringing-forth of new distinctions** — adjacent to the enactive `enactive-cognition` slug and the participatory-sense-making disposition (ADR-0063, a prose-only scope-condition on Signal, NOT a slug-admission). C7's "its own view of what is and what matters" and C8's Lorde reframe also resonate loosely with Johar's two modes-across-primitives, `expressive-power` (life articulating new meaning/perception) and `constructed-power` (situational agency emerging in response) (ADR-0048). These are loose cognitive-layer illustrations of already-canonical modes — **no canon-shift, no new pressure.**

### 4.4 Selectivity-as-power and model-answerability ↔ F4 representation-authority (ADR-0074 / ADR-0090)

C10's "selectivity… some data attended to, others ignored… applies to everything from natural language to sensors" is the **perception-as-power** territory: attention/visibility is itself a power-bearing act, and what a system declines to perceive shapes what it can coordinate over. This sits inside the F4 representation-authority **perception-as-power scope-condition** (ADR-0090; a scope-condition, NOT a slug). Separately, C3's "adjust the details of our models in response to the data" is the cognitive cousin of F4's evidence-vs-specification precedence (ADR-0074): models are derived projections answerable to what is sensed.

### 4.5 Learning-from-error and response ↔ F6 failure-modes / F5 actuator-logic (ADR-0075 / ADR-0076)

C2's "it won't generally repeat them" and C4's catalogue of institutions that "lack even basic learning loops" parallel Spore F6 failure-modes (ADR-0075): the capacity to **recognize and categorize** failure (`failure-mode-class`) is what prevents repetition. C3/C5's "act and observe when the world does or does not respond as expected, and adjust" is the cognitive analogue of F5 actuator-logic (ADR-0076): an **observable epistemic gap** between model and sensed reality is precisely what triggers a response. Descriptive isomorphisms against existing canon.

### 4.6 What does NOT resonate (honest non-mapping)

Mulgan's "no optimum balance between the loops… knowable only in retrospect" (C11) and the exploration/exploitation trade-off (C6) are genuinely **open** in Spore's grammar — Spore canon does not commit to a balancing rule between conservation and revision, and `canon-coverage-gaps` honestly does not claim to. This is a *non-mapping*, not a gap-to-fill: it is a strategy/balance question, which Spore treats as instance-level (operator/federation judgment), not a canon-doctrine. Noted descriptively to avoid over-claiming resonance.
